The True Size Of: drag a country, see how big it really is

By AiCoN | Published 22 October 2026 | Sources checked 22 October 2026

Pick up a country and drop it somewhere else on the map: the displayed outline stretches or shrinks to match the local map scale, while the country's actual area never changes. That is the whole idea, and it is free, needs no sign-up, and works in the browser. Here is what the official pages confirm and what our hands-on test with India found.

Quick facts

  • What it is: a free interactive world map that corrects the size distortion of the familiar Mercator map. Search a country or state, then drag its outline anywhere on the map to compare true sizes at a glance.
  • Cost: completely free. No account, no app, no paywall. The site runs on donations (Buy Us a Coffee) and advertising (see Privacy below).
  • Made by: James Talmage and Damon Maneice, inspired by an episode of The West Wing and Kai Krause's "The True Size of Africa" infographic.
  • Works on: modern web browsers. Nothing to install. (We tested on desktop.)
  • Languages: 15, including Hindi.
  • Data: Natural Earth, OpenHistoricalMap, Historical Basemaps, Native Land Digital, Wikidata and World Bank Open Data, on a Google Maps base, per the site's About page.
  • Link: thetruesize.com

Why a "true size" map?

The world map most of us grew up with uses the Mercator projection, which stretches countries near the poles and shrinks ones near the equator. The site's own About page puts it plainly: on a Mercator map, Greenland looks roughly the same size as Africa. The same page gives the figures: Greenland is 2.16 million km² and Africa is 30.37 million km². Dividing those numbers, Africa is about 14 times larger; the page itself rounds this to "nearly 14 and a half times". The True Size Of lets you check the effect yourself by picking countries up and moving them around.

Our hands-on test (22 October 2026)

We opened thetruesize.com in a desktop browser and tried the core feature ourselves:

  • Typing "India" into the search box brought up India itself, the historical British Raj, and Indian states including Assam, Karnataka, Kerala, Meghalaya and Punjab.
  • Selecting India dropped a pink outline of the country onto the map, with a card showing 1,269,218 sq mi of area and a 1.5 billion population figure.
  • We dragged the India outline from its real position up over Russia and Central Asia using real mouse movements. The displayed outline visibly grew as it moved north, while the stated area stayed the same: the Mercator effect in reverse, happening live under the cursor.
  • A unit toggle switches between square miles and km², and a time slider runs from 1858 to the present, which is how historical entries like the British Raj appear.

Everything above worked as described, with no sign-up at any point.

What you can use it for

  • Settle debates: is India really bigger than Greenland? Drag both onto the same spot and see.
  • Compare Indian states with whole countries.
  • Show students why flat world maps mislead. The makers say they built it hoping teachers would use it in class.
  • Explore how borders looked at different points between 1858 and today.

Limits to know

  • Area and population figures come from World Bank Open Data and Wikidata, and the site says historical boundaries are approximate and may be disputed. Treat it as an educational comparison, not a survey tool.
  • The base map is Google Maps, so it needs a working connection to Google's map servers.
  • We verified the search, outline and drag behaviour described above in one desktop session. We did not test every country or the mobile layout.

Privacy

The site's privacy note says it does not collect, store or share personal data directly from visitors, but two third parties may collect data: its advertising partner NitroPay (cookies and similar technologies for personalized ads) and Google Analytics (anonymized usage data). That is the provider's own policy statement; we did not audit their servers or network. In our test session no ad slots actually appeared on the page, but the site is ad-supported by design.

Access from India

The site loaded and worked normally in our test session, with no sign-up or gate encountered. We did not test from an Indian internet connection, so access from India is unverified. One plus for Indian users: the interface comes in 15 languages including Hindi.

The verdict

The True Size Of is that rare site that is both fun and genuinely educational. Thirty seconds of dragging India over to Greenland teaches you more about map distortion than a chapter of geography. It is free, instant and takes no sign-up: thetruesize.com
